# Authoritarian Exit Dilemmas and Democratic Transition: The Role of Credible Exit Guarantees in Conflict Prevention

**Author:** Dr. Fatih Sayin  
**Affiliation:** Foundation for Multilateral Strategies (FMS), www.fmsthinktank.org

---

## Abstract

**Purpose:** This article examines why authoritarian leaders frequently escalate repression and violence during political crises, and whether credible post-exit guarantees—negotiated transitions, exile arrangements, conditional amnesties, and transitional justice mechanisms—can reduce violence and facilitate democratic transition.

**Methodology:** The study develops the *Authoritarian Exit Dilemma* as a formal theoretical framework and applies comparative qualitative case analysis with process-tracing across eight cases: South Africa, Chile, Spain, Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, Syria, and Yemen.

**Findings:** Rulers who perceive political exit as existentially dangerous—risking imprisonment, exile, asset confiscation, or death—face incentives to escalate rather than negotiate. Where third parties credibly guarantee safe exit or sequenced accountability, violence tends to decline and transition becomes more feasible. Where guarantees are absent, contested, or withdrawn, repression intensifies and conflict protracts. The analysis identifies systematic trade-offs between impunity, justice, and stability.

**Originality:** The article formalizes the Authoritarian Exit Dilemma as a distinct causal mechanism linking elite survival calculations to conflict escalation, integrating authoritarian survival literature with transitional justice and political settlement theory.

**Implications:** Mediators, regional organizations, and the United Nations should treat exit-credible bargaining infrastructure—conditional amnesties, verified exile, security guarantees, and phased transitional justice—as preventive diplomacy tools, not moral concessions divorced from empirical transition dynamics.

**Keywords:** authoritarian exit dilemma; democratic transition; credible exit guarantees; transitional justice; political settlements; conflict prevention; elite bargaining; amnesty; exile; repression

## 1. Introduction

Authoritarian leaders facing mass mobilization, military defection, or negotiated transition rarely behave as rational agents indifferent to personal survival. A recurring pattern in twentieth- and twenty-first-century transitions is that rulers who perceive political exit as existentially dangerous—risking imprisonment, exile, asset confiscation, or death—choose escalation over accommodation. Libya's collapse after 2011, Syria's protracted civil war, and Yemen's fragmented conflict illustrate how absent or non-credible exit pathways can transform reformist moments into zero-sum violence. Conversely, South Africa's negotiated settlement, Chile's phased transition, Spain's post-Franco pact, and Tunisia's relatively low-cost removal of Ben Ali suggest that sequenced guarantees can lower the cost of stepping down.

This article addresses a research puzzle at the intersection of democratization, conflict prevention, and transitional justice: *Why do authoritarian leaders resist transition even when continuation appears costly, and under what conditions can third parties reduce violence by altering exit expectations?* Existing literature explains authoritarian durability (Geddes, 1999; Svolik, 2012), transition typologies (O'Donnell & Schmitter, 1986; Przeworski, 1991), and post-conflict justice (Hayner, 2011; Olsen et al., 2010), but often treats exit guarantees as background conditions rather than as causal variables shaping repression and bargaining.

The article makes three contributions. First, it defines and formalizes the **Authoritarian Exit Dilemma (AED)**: a decision structure in which declining regime security increases the perceived cost of exit relative to the cost of continued repression, producing escalation incentives when guarantees are not credible. Second, it applies comparative process-tracing across eight cases selected for variation in exit guarantee credibility, violence intensity, and democratic outcomes. Third, it derives policy implications for mediators and international organizations without treating justice and stability as mutually exclusive categories.

The empirical motivation extends beyond abstract regime typology. In the Middle East and North Africa (MENA), the 2011 uprisings exposed a recurring dynamic: incumbents who had closed institutional off-ramps for decades confronted mobilized societies with no credible bargain on the table. Where exile or conditional immunity was offered early—Tunisia—the human cost of leader removal remained comparatively contained. Where prosecution, ICC referral, or battlefield elimination was foregrounded—Libya and, in effect, Syria—the conflict arc shifted toward maximal violence. Egypt occupies an intermediate position: formal removal of Mubarak without guarantees to the coercive core produced a truncated transition and authoritarian restoration within three years. These patterns are not reducible to culture or religion; they align with elite survival calculus documented across regions (Brownlee, 2007; Howard & Roessler, 2006).

From a conflict prevention standpoint, the policy relevance is immediate. The Uppsala Conflict Data Program recorded fifty-nine state-based armed conflicts in 2023—the highest count in the post-1945 era (Uppsala University, 2024). A substantial share originate in or persist because of governance crises where incumbent removal becomes a zero-sum fight. Preventive diplomacy toolkits emphasize early warning and mediation, yet often under-specify the **guarantee architecture** required for rulers to accept stand-down. This article supplies that specification.

The analysis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews relevant literatures. Section 3 develops the AED framework and hypotheses. Section 4 describes methodology and case selection. Section 5 presents comparative case analysis. Sections 6–8 discuss findings, policy implications, and the justice–stability tension. Section 9 concludes.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Authoritarian Survival and Repression

Comparative authoritarianism treats regime survival as a strategic problem of cooptation, repression, and information control (Geddes, 1999; Gandhi & Przeworski, 2007). Svolik (2012) models dictators' core dilemma as managing elite threats while preventing mass rebellion. Levitsky and Way (2010) emphasize competitive authoritarianism, where incumbents manipulate institutions without fully abandoning pluralism. Hafner-Burton et al. (2018) document how repression intensity responds to perceived threat levels.

This literature explains *why* autocrats repress but less often specifies *when* repression becomes a function of personal exit expectations rather than collective regime maintenance. Roeder (1994) and Lustick (1994) analyze institutional stickiness and state breakdown, noting that personalist regimes collapse differently from party-based systems because leader removal threatens entire patronage networks. Mainwaring and Pérez-Liñán (2013) demonstrate that Latin American autocrats' survival strategies varied with opposition strength and economic performance, conditioning when exit became preferable to fight. The AED framework extends this insight by treating personal survival calculations as independent variables affecting violence escalation.

### 2.2 Democratic Transition Theory

Classic transition theory distinguishes paths from authoritarian rule: pact-based, imposed, and uncertain transitions (O'Donnell & Schmitter, 1986). Przeworski (1991) formalizes the "institutionalization dilemma": elites accept democracy only when they believe they can survive it electorally or physically. Karl (1990) and Linz and Stepan (1996) emphasize pact-making and civil society mobilization. Carothers (2002) later cautioned against teleological "transition paradigm" assumptions.

The AED framework aligns with Przeworski's insight that uncertainty about post-transition treatment blocks cooperation, but shifts focus from electoral confidence alone to *physical and legal* post-exit security. Where transition literature emphasizes elite splits and civil society pressure as triggers, this article specifies a mediating mechanism: credible guarantees convert existential uncertainty into negotiable risk.

### 2.3 Elite Bargaining and Political Settlements

Political settlement theory analyzes how dominant elites distribute power and resources to maintain order (Khan, 2010; Parks & Cole, 2010). Di John and Putzel (2009) link settlements to tax, security, and legitimacy bargains. Bell (2015) and Paffenholz et al. (2016) connect inclusive negotiations to peace durability.

Settlements fail when excluded actors reject terms or when guarantors lack enforcement capacity. The AED framework treats exit guarantees as settlement infrastructure: without credible third-party enforcement, personalist leaders rationally anticipate victors' justice and resist deals that appear reversible.

### 2.4 Transitional Justice, Amnesty, and Exile

Transitional justice scholarship examines truth commissions, trials, reparations, and institutional reform (Teitel, 2000; Hayner, 2011). Olsen et al. (2010) find mixed effects of justice mechanisms on human rights and democracy. Mallinder (2008) and Freeman (2009) analyze amnesty as a bargaining instrument rather than purely normative compromise. Snyder and Vinjamuri (2003) argue that pursuing accountability during ongoing conflict can intensify violence if it forecloses exit.

Exile and negotiated removal appear in diplomatic practice but receive less systematic comparative treatment. Kersten (2015) documents international exile arrangements; Bass (2000) examines war crimes prosecutions' deterrent and destabilizing effects. The present study integrates these strands by treating amnesty, exile, and sequenced prosecution as *credibility instruments* affecting AED resolution.

### 2.5 Conflict Prevention and Mediation

Preventive diplomacy literature emphasizes early intervention before violence hardens (United Nations, 2015; World Bank, 2017). Stedman et al. (2002) and Crocker et al. (2007) analyze mediation leverage and spoiler problems. De Waal (2015) critiques technocratic peacebuilding that ignores political marketplace dynamics.

Conflict prevention often frames accountability and peace as competing goods. The AED framework reframes the trade-off: guarantees are not moral endorsements of impunity but structural preconditions that may reduce aggregate harm when sequenced with later accountability, as in Chile and South Africa.

### 2.6 Synthesis: Gaps in the Literature

Three gaps motivate the AED framework. First, authoritarian survival models treat repression as regime-level strategy but under-theorize **personal exit cost** as a trigger of escalation. Second, transition theory emphasizes pacts and civil society without systematically coding guarantee credibility as an observable variable. Third, transitional justice debates polarize amnesty and prosecution rather than analyzing **sequencing** under ongoing threat. The present study integrates these literatures into a single causal framework testable through comparative process-tracing.

## 3. Theoretical Framework: The Authoritarian Exit Dilemma

### 3.1 Definition

The **Authoritarian Exit Dilemma** arises when a ruler or ruling coalition perceives that relinquishing power exposes them to outcomes worse than continued repression: imprisonment, forced exile under unsafe conditions, confiscation of assets, violent retaliation, or death. Under AED, the ruler faces a sequential game: at each crisis node, choose among (a) concession/negotiation, (b) limited repression, or (c) maximal violence to forestall removal.

The dilemma is *authoritarian* because it is most acute in personalist and patrimonial systems where institutional succession is weak and leader fate is tied to network survival (Geddes, 1999; Svolik, 2012). It is an *exit* dilemma because the blocking variable is not preference for democracy per se but expected post-exit treatment.

### 3.2 Variables and Causal Mechanisms

**Independent variable:** credibility of post-exit guarantees (*G*), composed of:

- *G₁* Legal immunity or conditional amnesty enforceability  
- *G₂* Verified exile with host-state and international monitoring  
- *G₃* Asset protection or sequenced forfeiture rules  
- *G₄* Security guarantees for family and coalition members  
- *G₅* Credible sequencing to accountability (e.g., truth commission before prosecution)

**Dependent variables:**

- *V* Violence intensity (state repression, civil war battle deaths)  
- *T* Transition outcome (democratic consolidation, authoritarian restoration, state collapse)  
- *D* Duration of conflict or stalemate

**Mediating mechanisms:**

1. **Existential risk amplification:** Low *G* raises perceived cost of exit → increases willingness to use force.  
2. **Spoiler consolidation:** Uncertainty binds security elites to the leader ("coup trap"), reducing internal pressure to negotiate (Svolik, 2012).  
3. **Audience costs inversion:** Public trials or ICC threats intended to deter abuse may signal non-negotiability to incumbents (Snyder & Vinjamuri, 2003).  
4. **Credible sequencing:** Partial guarantees with phased accountability reduce short-run existential risk while preserving long-run justice (South Africa, Chile).

**Moderating factors:** regime type (personalist vs. military vs. single-party), external patron support, military cohesion, international mediator capacity, and civil society mobilization strength.

### 3.3 Hypotheses

**H1:** Lower credibility of post-exit guarantees (*G*) is associated with higher violence intensity (*V*) during crisis periods.

**H2:** Credible sequenced guarantees increase the probability of negotiated transition (*T*) without full state collapse.

**H3:** Unconditional impunity maximizes short-term violence reduction but correlates with weaker democratic consolidation and higher long-term recurrence risk.

**H4:** Withdrawn or deceptive guarantees (promised exile followed by prosecution or extradition) increase distrust and prolong conflict in subsequent crises.

Figure 1 summarizes the causal architecture.

**Figure 1.** Authoritarian Exit Dilemma — Causal Pathways

| Stage | Condition | Expected outcome |
|-------|-----------|------------------|
| Crisis onset | Mass mobilization or elite defection | Ruler assesses exit cost |
| Low *G* | No credible amnesty/exile/security | Escalation (*V* ↑); stalemate or collapse |
| Medium *G* | Sequenced guarantees + external enforcement | Negotiated transition; phased *T* |
| High impunity | Total immunity, no accountability sequencing | Low immediate *V*; weak rule-of-law *T* |
| Guarantee betrayal | Exit promise revoked | *H4*: future non-cooperation; *V* ↑ in later episodes |

### 3.4 Scope Conditions

AED applies most directly to personalist and patrimonial authoritarian regimes facing internal challenge. It is less determinative in military juntas with institutional succession rules, or in external regime-change wars where leader exit is militarily imposed. The framework is probabilistic: guarantees are neither sufficient nor always necessary, but they systematically alter incentives.

### 3.5 Observable Indicators

Operationalizing *G* for case analysis requires transparent indicators: (1) written or publicly acknowledged amnesty or immunity provisions; (2) formal exile agreements with host-state recognition; (3) third-party guarantor statements (UN, AU, GCC, regional powers); (4) absence of ICC referral or explicit prosecution commitments during negotiation windows; (5) subsequent enforcement or reversal of guarantees. Violence (*V*) is assessed via battle deaths, Political Terror Scale scores, and documented security-force fatalities during defined crisis windows. Transition (*T*) is coded from regime type indices and expert assessments at five-year horizons post-crisis onset.

## 4. Methodology

### 4.1 Research Design

The study employs a **comparative qualitative case study** design with **process-tracing** (Bennett & Checkel, 2015; George & Bennett, 2005). The objective is not statistical generalization but identification of causal mechanisms linking exit guarantee credibility to violence and transition outcomes across structurally comparable episodes of authoritarian crisis.

### 4.2 Case Selection

Eight cases were selected using **most similar systems** and **variation on the independent variable** logic:

| Case | Crisis period | Exit guarantee profile | Violence level | Transition outcome |
|------|---------------|------------------------|----------------|-------------------|
| South Africa | 1990–1994 | High sequenced (*G₅* TRC; elite pact) | Moderate (low civil war) | Democratic consolidation |
| Chile | 1988–1990+ | Medium sequenced (Pinochet immunity contested) | Low post-plebiscite | Gradual democracy; delayed accountability |
| Spain | 1975–1978 | High impunity (Amnesty Law; pact of forgetting) | Very low | Stable democracy; delayed justice |
| Tunisia | 2010–2014 | Medium (*G₂* Ben Ali exile to Saudi Arabia) | Low | Competitive authoritarian relapse |
| Egypt | 2011–2013 | Low (*G* absent; Mubarak prosecuted) | Moderate | Authoritarian restoration |
| Libya | 2011 | None (*G* = 0; ICC; military defeat) | Very high | State collapse |
| Syria | 2011–present | None (Assad survival strategy) | Very high | Protracted civil war |
| Yemen | 2011–present | Low/contested (Saleh GCC deal violated) | Very high | Fragmented conflict |

Cases span four regions and vary on *G*, *V*, and *T*, enabling within-mechanism comparison. Middle Eastern cases (Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, Syria, Yemen) anchor contemporary relevance; Southern European and Southern African cases provide successful and sequenced comparators.

### 4.3 Data and Evidence

Evidence draws on secondary historiography, truth commission reports, UN mediation documents, human rights datasets (UCDP; Political Terror Scale), and peer-reviewed democratization studies. Process-tracing examines decision points: ruler statements, mediator proposals, amnesty/exile agreements, and subsequent enforcement or reversal.

Following Bennett and Checkel (2015), each case applies **hoop tests** (guarantee absence predicts escalation) and **smoking gun probes** (documents or statements linking ruler decisions explicitly to exit fear). For South Africa, archival evidence of National Party negotiators' immunity concerns constitutes a smoking gun; for Libya, ICC referral timing relative to mediation contacts serves a similar function. Negative cases—Spain's low violence despite Francoist elite continuity—are analyzed for scope conditions rather than falsification.

### 4.4 Analytic Narrative Standards

Analytic narratives follow George and Bennett's (2005) standards: explicit causal claims, temporal ordering, and consideration of alternative explanations (economic shock, ethnic cleavage, external intervention). Where confounders dominate—Syria's internationalized war—AED claims are limited to **leader-level bargaining constraints** rather than full war termination.

### 4.5 Limitations

Case selection limits external validity. Measurement of *G* credibility involves judgment about counterfactual enforcement. Confounding variables—oil rents, geopolitical intervention, ethnic fragmentation—are addressed through within-case plausibility probes rather than eliminated statistically.

## 5. Comparative Case Analysis

### 5.1 South Africa: Sequenced Guarantees and Negotiated Transition

South Africa approximates the **medium–high sequenced guarantee** model. De Klerk's 1990 reforms occurred amid ongoing violence, but National Party elites received implicit assurances that wholesale victors' justice would not follow immediately. The 1993 interim constitution and 1995 Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) institutionalized conditional amnesty: disclosure in exchange for legal immunity (Hayner, 2011; Gibson, 2004). Violence did not disappear—Boipatong, Chris Hani's assassination, and IFP–ANC conflict continued—but existential exit costs for regime elites fell sufficiently to sustain talks.

Process-tracing supports **H2**: sequenced *G₅* reduced incentives for security-sector scorched-earth resistance compared to scenarios where prosecution was immediate and certain. De Klerk's calculus explicitly referenced fears of revolutionary justice; ANC leadership, conversely, required assurance that apartheid security chiefs would not annihilate the transition (Gibson, 2004). The TRC's conditional amnesty—Applicants had to disclose political crimes fully; amnesty was not automatic for all offenses—created a middle path between victors' courts and blanket immunity.

**H3** partially applies: delayed accountability aided consolidation but left structural inequality and impunity grievances among victims who viewed elite pact as transactional. South Africa demonstrates that guarantees need not be morally unlimited to be politically effective, but sequenced design must retain victim voice to preserve legitimacy (Hayner, 2011).

### 5.2 Chile: Plebiscite Transition and Contested Immunity

Chile combines institutional exit (1988 plebiscite loss) with **contested medium guarantees**. Pinochet retained army command until 1998; the 1978 amnesty and transitional calendar limited immediate prosecution. Repression declined sharply post-1990, supporting **H1** contrast with cases lacking any guarantee. Subsequent legal challenges—London arrest, domestic trials—illustrate **sequenced accountability** without initial existential blockage.

Pinochet's eventual accountability also shows **H4** risk management: guarantees were political understandings as much as legal certainties. The 1980 constitution's transitional provisions and military autonomy delayed subordination to civilian courts until democratic institutions strengthened—a sequencing choice that human rights organizations criticized but that plausibly prevented armed resistance by the Carabineros and army factions (Linz & Stepan, 1996).

Chile's relatively stable democracy suggests medium *G* with delayed justice can succeed, though human rights constituencies debate the moral cost. The case underscores that **electoral exit** (plebiscite defeat) differs from **physical security exit**; Pinochet retained coercive leverage after losing the vote, requiring continued guarantee management.

### 5.3 Spain: Impunity-Maximizing Pact and Democratic Consolidation

Spain's post-Franco transition prioritized **high impunity** via the 1977 Amnesty Law and elite pact of *olvido*. Violence remained minimal; democratic institutions consolidated rapidly (Linz & Stepan, 1996). This supports **H3**: unconditional impunity correlated with low immediate *V* and successful *T*, but decades of delayed justice for Franco-era crimes illustrate long-run legitimacy deficits.

Spain is often treated as exceptional—European integration, homogenous elite consensus, Francoist technocratic continuity—but its exit logic aligns with AED: reformist Francoists and democrats mutually feared uncontrollable retribution without amnesty. The Moncloa Pact negotiations explicitly traded political inclusion for legal forgetting, demonstrating that **elite fear of exit costs** operated even where mass violence was absent (Linz & Stepan, 1996). Spain thus supports AED's claim that guarantee politics precedes violence outcomes rather than merely responding to them.

The 2007 Historical Memory Law and later exhumation politics illustrate delayed justice pressures—consistent with **H3** long-run trade-offs between stability and accountability.

### 5.4 Tunisia: Exile Without Institutional Sequencing

Ben Ali's January 2011 flight to Saudi Arabia exemplifies **medium *G₂*** exile guarantee. Short-term violence was low relative to neighbors; Tunisia initiated constitutional reform and elections. However, absent robust *G₅* sequencing and economic inclusion, competitive authoritarian relapse under Saied (2021) suggests **H3**: exile alone insufficient for durable *T*.

Tunisia contrasts with Egypt and Libya on the same timeline, supporting AED's emphasis on exit pathways during acute crisis moments. The Ennahda–secular party bargains, constitution-drafting process, and relative restraint of the military further distinguish Tunisia from Egypt's coup trajectory—yet Saied's 2021 power concentration indicates that **leader exile without institutional guarantee sequencing** leaves backsliding pathways open (Aksoy & Özler, 2020).

### 5.5 Egypt: Prosecution Signal and Authoritarian Restoration

Mubarak's prosecution, military management of transition, and absence of credible elite guarantees align with **low *G***. Though initial violence was limited, the military–Muslim Brotherhood confrontation escalated after 2013; Sisi's consolidation restored authoritarianism. Process-tracing indicates security elites feared accountability and Islamist dominance—existential framing blocked pact expansion.

Egypt supports **H1**: without credible guarantees to cohesive coercive actors, transition stalls and repression returns even after leader removal. Human Rights Watch (2012) documented escalating security-force abuses after 2011; the military's material interests—economic empire, legal autonomy—were never addressed in a comprehensive settlement. Mubarak's trial signaled accountability to protesters but simultaneously communicated to generals that **no safe institutional exit** existed, reinforcing AED predictions.

### 5.6 Libya: Zero Guarantee and State Collapse

NATO intervention, ICC referral, and Qaddafi's battlefield death closed exit options. Personalist network members faced elimination or flight without negotiated sequencing. Violence spiked; state institutions fragmented (International Crisis Group, 2011). Libya is a strong **H1** case: *G* ≈ 0 correlated with maximal *V* and collapse *T*.

Counterfactual claims require caution, but mediator accounts suggest Qaddafi envoys explored exit deals rejected by rebels and external actors prioritizing accountability and regime change (Kersten, 2015). The ICC Prosecutor's February 2011 referral intensified exit costs at precisely the moment when preventive bargaining might have been leveraged—a policy tension central to **H4** and to debates on complementarity (Bass, 2000; Rome Statute, 1998).

Libya also illustrates **patronage network collapse**: guarantees extended only to Qaddafi personally would likely have been insufficient without parallel assurances to tribal and security constituencies—a design lesson for mediators.

### 5.7 Syria: Survival Through Absent Exit and Externalization

Assad's strategy assumed no credible *G* and high cost of exit— reinforced by Libya precedent and ICC discourse. External patrons (Russia, Iran) altered military balance, prolonging stalemate. Syria represents AED failure at scale: low *G*, high *V*, indefinite conflict. Opposition fragmentation and geopolitical proxy war add confounders, but ruler exit expectations remained central to bargaining deadlocks. UN Special Envoy proposals repeatedly foundered on Assad's insistence on survival and opposition rejection of amnesty; the 2014 Geneva communiqué's transitional body formula lacked credible *G* architecture (Stedman et al., 2002). External patrons altered military balance but did not substitute for domestic exit guarantees—Russia's 2015 intervention reduced regime collapse risk, reinforcing AED's **low exit cost of fighting** relative to negotiated removal.

Syria also demonstrates **network AED**: guarantees must cover coercive and economic elites, not only the presidential core, when patrimonial structures distribute survival stakes across militias and business cronies (Wright, 2015).

### 5.8 Yemen: Violated Guarantee and Spoiler Dynamics

President Saleh accepted a 2011 GCC initiative providing immunity and phased transition, illustrating attempted **medium *G***. The immunity law passed by Yemen's parliament appeared to satisfy **G₁**, while Gulf host states offered informal **G₂** assurances. Saleh's return, Houthi expansion, and Saudi-led intervention demonstrate **H4**: guarantee credibility collapsed when enforcement failed and spoilers re-entered. Saleh's 2017 death amid factional fighting underscores the personal stakes when guarantees unravel.

Yemen combines low effective *G* with extreme *V* and fragmented *T*. Refugee outflows and humanitarian catastrophe further illustrate aggregate costs when AED remains unresolved (Zolberg, 1989). The case cautions that **partial guarantees to single actors** without broader settlement inclusion generate spoiler incentives.

Comparatively, the eight cases cluster predictably: high or sequenced *G* associates with lower peak violence and more structured transitions (South Africa, Chile, Spain, Tunisia acutely); absent or betrayed *G* associates with escalation or collapse (Libya, Syria, Yemen; Egypt restoration).

Cross-case comparison also reveals **asymmetric learning**: Syrian and Yemeni elites observed Libyan and Egyptian outcomes and updated exit expectations downward, reinforcing fight-to-the-end strategies independent of immediate guarantee offers. This feedback dynamic—visible in mediator memoirs and leader statements—extends AED temporally: guarantee credibility is path-dependent across cases in the same region (De Waal, 2015).

## 6. Findings

Analysis across cases yields four patterned findings.

**Finding 1: Exit guarantee credibility inversely correlates with escalation incentives.** Where rulers and coercive elites believe exit is survivable, they accept pacts that appear politically costly. Where exit implies death or humiliation, maximal force becomes rational at the individual level even if collectively destructive.

**Finding 2: Sequenced accountability outperforms both unconditional impunity and immediate prosecution during active crisis.** South Africa and Chile show that phased truth-telling, conditional amnesty, and delayed courts can unlock negotiation without permanently foreclosing justice. Spain shows impunity's stability benefits but also its long-run normative costs.

**Finding 3: Exile guarantees require enforceable host-state and international monitoring.** Tunisia's low immediate violence contrasts with Saleh's manipulated immunity in Yemen. **H4** is confirmed: deceptive or unenforced guarantees poison later mediation.

**Finding 4: Guarantees are necessary but not sufficient for democratic consolidation.** Economic inclusion, military subordination to civilian rule, and party system design mediate long-run *T*. Tunisia and Egypt demonstrate that removing a leader without restructuring coercive institutions permits authoritarian restoration.

Impunity risks are real: guarantees can entrench corruption, weaken rule of law, and signal elite exemption (Olsen et al., 2010). Justice–peace trade-offs are context-dependent rather than fixed moral binaries (Snyder & Vinjamuri, 2003; Freeman, 2009).

**Table 2** summarizes cross-case pattern consistency with hypotheses.

| Hypothesis | Supported cases | Partial / mixed | Contradictory |
|------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| H1 (low *G* → high *V*) | Libya, Syria, Yemen, Egypt (post-2013) | Tunisia (short window) | Spain (high impunity, low *V*) |
| H2 (sequenced *G* → negotiated *T*) | South Africa, Chile | Tunisia | — |
| H3 (total impunity → weak rule-of-law *T*) | Spain | Chile, South Africa | Tunisia (exile only) |
| H4 (betrayed *G* → prolonged conflict) | Yemen, Libya (post-referral) | Chile (delayed prosecution) | — |

Spain's high impunity-low violence pairing does not contradict **H1** because *G* was high (immunity credible), not absent; violence was low precisely when exit costs were managed—consistent with AED scope conditions.

## 7. Policy Implications

For the United Nations, regional organizations, and mediators, AED implies treating exit infrastructure as **conflict prevention architecture**, subject to sequencing and verification.

**Conditional amnesties.** Frameworks should distinguish blanket impunity from conditional amnesty tied to truth disclosure, disarmament, and institutional reform—mirroring South Africa's TRC model and ICC Article 53 discretion on complementarity (Mallinder, 2008; Rome Statute, 1998).

**Verified exile.** Host states and regional bodies should formalize exile agreements with monitoring, preventing arbitrary extradition reversal unless new crimes occur post-agreement (Kersten, 2015).

**Security guarantees.** Mediators must extend protections to families and mid-level coercive actors, not only apex leaders, to prevent coup-trap dynamics (Svolik, 2012).

**Transitional justice sequencing.** Truth commissions, reparations, and vetting can precede prosecutions where immediate trials would foreclose negotiation (Hayner, 2011; Freeman, 2009).

**Preventive diplomacy timing.** Guarantees are most leverage-rich at crisis apex—before battle deaths accumulate and reputational costs of compromise rise (United Nations, 2015; World Bank, 2017).

**Conditionality and review.** Agreements should include sunset clauses, judicial review mechanisms, and civil society consultation to mitigate **H3** impunity costs.

Policy design should avoid public maximalist rhetoric that inadvertently signals non-negotiability to incumbents while preserving long-run accountability pathways.

**Regional organizations.** The African Union's refusal to cooperate with the ICC in certain cases reflects, in part, AED logic among member incumbents who perceive prosecution as existential threat. The Gulf Cooperation Council's Yemen initiative demonstrates both potential and failure modes of regional guarantee offers when enforcement is weak (International Crisis Group, 2011).

**Transitional governments.** Post-transition authorities should codify guarantee sequencing in constitutional transitional clauses—mandating truth commissions before prosecutorial waves—to prevent arbitrary reversal and to signal credible *G₅* to holdout factions.

**Monitoring and verification.** Independent panels—akin to peace agreement verification missions—should track compliance with exile, asset, and immunity terms, reporting publicly to reduce **H4** betrayal risk.

## 8. Discussion

The AED framework clarifies tensions among justice, accountability, stability, and conflict prevention. Prosecutorial absolutism during active conflict may increase human suffering if it eliminates off-ramps (Snyder & Vinjamuri, 2003). Conversely, unconditional impunity may stabilize transitions short-term while undermining rule-of-law consolidation (Teitel, 2000; Olsen et al., 2010).

Normatively, scholars and practitioners need not treat guarantees as endorsement of authoritarian crime. Empirically, they function as **risk management instruments** in nested games where multiple elite factions calculate survival. Ethical policy therefore emphasizes sequencing, conditionality, and inclusive victim participation rather than binary amnesty-versus-justice frames.

Comparative limits remain. External military intervention, as in Libya, transforms AED into wartime elimination logic distinct from negotiated civilian transitions. Geopolitical patrons can subsidize fight-to-the-death strategies regardless of guarantee offers, as in Syria. Future research should test AED quantitatively using cross-national datasets on amnesty laws, exile events, and repression intensity, and examine gendered and communal dimensions of exit bargaining excluded here.

Integration with democratic transition theory suggests revisiting Przeworski's institutionalization dilemma: physical survival clauses may be as consequential as electoral confidence in personalist regimes. Integration with political settlement theory positions exit guarantees as settlement public goods requiring third-party enforcement.

Comparative historical scope reinforces the framework. Southern European transitions in the 1970s, Latin American exits from military rule, and Southern Africa's negotiated settlements all confronted AED logic, albeit with different institutional inheritances. The MENA cases test whether AED mechanisms hold when sectarian fragmentation, external intervention, and resource dependence amplify confounders. Process-tracing suggests they do at the **leader and coercive-core** level, even when macro-level war persists.

Ethical objections to guaranteeing exit for perpetrators remain legitimate. The framework does not resolve them; it clarifies that **refusing all guarantees during active crisis carries its own moral cost** in expected civilian casualties—a consequentialist tension policymakers must weigh transparently rather than through absolutist rhetoric alone (Freeman, 2009; Snyder & Vinjamuri, 2003).

## 9. Conclusion

Authoritarian leaders often escalate repression and violence because they perceive political exit as existentially dangerous. The Authoritarian Exit Dilemma formalizes this incentive structure and links it to observable variation in conflict intensity and transition outcomes. Comparative analysis of South Africa, Chile, Spain, Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, Syria, and Yemen indicates that credible exit guarantees—negotiated transitions, exile arrangements, conditional amnesties, and sequenced transitional justice—can reduce violence and facilitate democratic transition when enforced and sequenced. Their absence or betrayal correlates with escalation, collapse, or protracted war.

The policy challenge is not choosing between justice and peace but designing **credible sequences** that reduce immediate harm without foreclosing accountability. Mediators and international organizations should invest in verifiable guarantee mechanisms as preventive tools, evaluating them against empirical transition dynamics rather than abstract moral categories alone.

Further research should refine measurement of guarantee credibility, examine regional organization roles, and assess long-run recurrence rates under alternative sequencing models. Quantitative extensions could code amnesty provisions from the Transitional Justice Database jointly with UCDP battle deaths and V-Dem liberal democracy indices, testing **H1** cross-nationally. Qualitative extensions should examine gendered harms when amnesties exclude sexual violence accountability, and communal conflicts where exit guarantees to one faction alienate others.

The central theoretical claim—that exit expectations shape repression choices—offers a durable lens for analyzing authoritarian crises in an era of international criminal law, social media mobilization, and renewed great-power competition. For scholars of democratization and conflict prevention alike, the Authoritarian Exit Dilemma reframes a recurrent policy question: not whether to "help those in wrong," in moralistic terms, but whether **credible, sequenced exit architecture** can reduce violence and open pathways to accountable governance when rulers face the abyss of irreversible defeat.

## 1. Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is reshaping the practice of international relations at a pace that outstrips the development of norms, institutions, and accountability mechanisms. When deployed with human dignity, transparency, and multilateral oversight at its center, AI-powered diplomacy can extend the reach of preventive diplomacy, sharpen early-warning systems, and create new channels for de-escalation before violence becomes irreversible. When deployed without such guardrails, the same technologies risk accelerating misperception, automating coercion, and widening the gap between powerful states and marginalized communities [1][2].

This FMS Strategic Review synthesizes independent analyses, three rounds of structured expert debate, cross-agent ethical and security challenges, and consensus-building across thirteen specialized peace agents. The collective assessment is cautiously optimistic but institutionally demanding: **AI can materially strengthen preventive diplomacy and sustainable peacebuilding, but only if it is subordinated to existing international law, anchored in human rights, and governed through inclusive multilateral frameworks rather than unilateral military or commercial advantage.**

Five strategic conclusions emerge:

1. **Prevention first.** AI's highest peace dividend lies in early warning, scenario modeling, and back-channel facilitation *before* armed conflict—not in automating battlefield decisions or information warfare [3][4].
2. **Human-in-the-loop diplomacy.** Negotiation, recognition, and trust-building remain irreducibly human; AI should augment preparation, translation, and pattern detection, not replace accountability for commitments [5].
3. **Governance parity.** Developing states, civil society, and conflict-affected populations must participate in setting standards for diplomatic AI, or tools will encode existing power asymmetries [6][7].
4. **Dual-use discipline.** Capabilities developed for humanitarian prediction or treaty verification can be repurposed for surveillance and targeting; export controls, auditing, and ethics review are essential [8][9].
5. **Implementation sequencing.** Near-term investments should prioritize shared data standards, UN-led pilot programs, and independent evaluation—while long-term architecture envisions federated diplomatic AI commons governed under Chapter VI/VII reform discussions [10][11].

The panel's integrated recommendation is to establish a **Global Diplomatic AI Compact (GDAIC)**—a voluntary but verifiable multilateral framework linking UN agencies, regional organizations, and member states to shared principles, audit protocols, and pilot deployments in preventive diplomacy. Confidence in this pathway is moderate (consensus confidence score: 0.60 in orchestrator merge) given unresolved tensions between security secrecy and transparency, and between speed of innovation and pace of norm-building.

**Historical context.** Previous technological revolutions in diplomacy—from the telegraph to satellite verification—expanded both cooperation and coercion [5][16]. AI differs in speed, opacity, and scalability: models can be updated weekly, while treaties take years. The international community therefore faces a **norm-building window** that may close within this decade as proprietary systems become entrenched in foreign ministries and intelligence agencies [1][2]. Missing that window risks a bifurcated order in which a few states operate AI-augmented diplomacy at machine speed while others negotiate blind—a recipe for miscalculation and war by misunderstanding.

**Scope of this review.** The question is not whether AI will touch diplomacy; it already does, through machine translation, open-source intelligence fusion, and social media monitoring. The question is whether the international community can **steer** that contact toward prevention and sustainable peace, with human dignity as the non-negotiable metric of success. The sections that follow map expert findings, debate tensions, risk and impact assessments, governance recommendations, phased actions, and a consensus strategy grounded in the orchestrator workflow and enriched by domain expertise where mock outputs required substantive synthesis.

## 2. Agent Findings (All 13)

### 2.1 Chief Peace Architect

As integrative chair, the Chief Peace Architect frames AI-powered diplomacy as a **system-of-systems** challenge: technology, institutions, and human dignity must advance together or not at all. The Chief proposes a three-layer architecture: (a) *operational layer*—tools for mediators and special envoys; (b) *institutional layer*—UN and regional organization capacity; (c) *normative layer*—binding and soft-law guardrails aligned with the UN Charter [10].

The Chief rejects both techno-utopianism ("AI will end war") and techno-fatalism ("AI inevitably accelerates conflict"). Instead, the integrative path treats diplomatic AI as **public infrastructure**—like air traffic control or epidemiological surveillance—where shared standards and inspection matter more than any single state's lead model. In practice, that means the UN should not attempt to monopolize all diplomatic AI development, but should **certify and host** neutral instances used in mandated mediation, while member states retain national systems subject to Compact obligations when engaging UN processes [11].

Key finding: fragmented national AI strategies will produce incompatible diplomatic systems, increasing miscalculation risk when crisis alerts, threat scores, or automated draft communiqués cross borders without shared semantics. Recommendation: designate a UN Secretary-General's Envoy on AI and International Peace to coordinate pilots, maintain a public registry of AI systems used in UN-led processes, and report annually to the Security Council and General Assembly on incidents, near-misses, and compliance trends [11].

### 2.2 Peace & Conflict Resolution

The Peace & Conflict expert emphasizes **peace processes, not just crisis management**. AI can map conflict economies, track implementation of ceasefire agreements through multimodal verification, and model power-sharing scenarios—but cannot substitute for the political will to compromise [12].

Key finding: algorithmic bias in historical conflict data can reproduce exclusion of women, youth, and minority groups from settlement design [13]. Recommendation: mandate inclusive training data review and local validation for any AI tool used in formal mediation support.

### 2.3 Diplomacy & International Relations

The Diplomacy & IR expert stresses **treaty literacy and forum strategy**. Large language models can accelerate legal and diplomatic text analysis, identify drafting ambiguities, and support multilingual negotiation—but diplomatic privilege and Article 2(7) sensitivities limit open-data approaches [5][14]. The expert notes that the most successful uses of technology in diplomacy historically—such as the P5+1 negotiation support structures or ASEAN's crisis communications—combined **discrete human trust** with **structured information management**, not automated bargaining.

AI can help smaller missions punch above their weight in complex multilateral forums by surfacing precedent, voting patterns, and draft language alternatives. The danger is **dependency without understanding**: diplomats who accept model-generated text without mastering underlying legal commitments may sign undertakings their capitals cannot implement, eroding trust and renewing conflict [14]. Forum strategy therefore requires "explainable diplomacy"—every AI-generated paragraph linked to sources, precedents, and identified political trade-offs.

Key finding: AI-enabled "virtual embassies" and digital negotiation platforms could democratize access to diplomatic education while risking surveillance of smaller states' positions if hosted on foreign commercial clouds. Recommendation: establish OECD/UNDP-style capacity-building funds for diplomatic AI literacy in the Global South; require regional organization custody of negotiation platforms for sensitive talks [15].

### 2.4 Strategic & Security Studies

The Strategic & Security expert warns that **AI compresses decision timelines** in crisis settings, reducing room for human judgment [16]. Military applications of AI (autonomous systems, ISR fusion) create spillover effects that contaminate trust in purely civilian diplomatic tools.

Key finding: adversarial use of deepfakes and synthetic media can undermine confidence in authentic diplomatic communications [17]. Recommendation: develop NATO-UN technical standards for cryptographic provenance of official state communications and hotline messages.

### 2.5 Humanitarian Affairs

The Humanitarian expert centers **civilian protection and IHL compliance**. AI can improve needs forecasting, supply chain optimization, and access negotiation modeling—but predictive models must not label populations as threats or justify withholding aid [18][19].

Key finding: integration of humanitarian data into diplomatic AI without consent risks violating humanitarian principles of independence and impartiality. Recommendation: firewall humanitarian datasets from security-classified diplomatic AI pipelines; adopt ICRC-guided impact assessments [20].

### 2.6 AI for Peace

The AI for Peace expert advocates **peace technology with verifiable impact**. Open-source tooling, red-team exercises, and public benchmarks for "diplomatic AI safety" can align innovation with de-escalation metrics rather than engagement metrics [21]. The expert distinguishes three application classes: *(i) analytic support* (pattern detection, legal research); *(ii) communicative support* (translation, drafting assistance); and *(iii) relational support* (sentiment mapping, trust-network analysis in negotiations). Only the first two are mature enough for near-term UN deployment; relational support demands extraordinary care to avoid manipulation [21].

Red-teaming should be adversarial and public where possible: test whether models escalate language under stress prompts, whether they reproduce stereotypes about ethnic groups, and whether they leak training data from confidential negotiations. PeaceTech networks should publish leaderboards ranking systems on **de-escalation scores**, not fluency alone [21].

Key finding: foundation models fine-tuned on classified or corporate data may be unsuitable for neutral mediation support. Recommendation: fund public-interest diplomatic foundation models trained on public international law corpora and de-identified mediation case studies; require annual third-party safety audits for any model used in UN peacemaking [12][21].

### 2.7 Economic Development

The Economic Development expert links **inclusive growth to conflict prevention**. AI-driven trade analysis, sanctions impact modeling, and post-conflict economic scenario planning can inform negotiations on reconstruction and resource governance [22].

Key finding: algorithmic sanctions enforcement can cause over-compliance and humanitarian harm. Recommendation: embed economic rights impact review in AI-assisted sanctions monitoring systems.

### 2.8 Civilization & Cultural Dialogue

The Civilization & Cultural Dialogue expert highlights **identity, memory, and reconciliation**. AI translation and cultural heritage digitization can bridge communities—but also flatten nuance or propagate culturally insensitive outputs [23].

Key finding: diplomatic AI must be evaluated for cultural humility, not only accuracy. Recommendation: require cultural advisors and diaspora consultation in tool deployment for intercommunal negotiations.

### 2.9 Education & Youth Empowerment

The Education & Youth expert focuses on **CVE prevention and civic agency**. AI tutors, peace education platforms, and youth diplomatic simulations can build long-term resilience against mobilization to violence [24].

Key finding: youth data protection is often weaker in ed-tech peace programs. Recommendation: adopt UNICEF-inspired child data governance for all AI peace education initiatives.

### 2.10 Media & Strategic Communication

The Media & Comms expert addresses **narrative integrity and information environments**. AI can counter hate speech and disinformation at scale—but also amplify propaganda if deployed without editorial ethics [17][25].

Key finding: "information peacekeeping" requires coordination between platforms, states, and civil society, not unilateral algorithmic censorship. Recommendation: support independent fact-checking networks with diplomatic AI interfaces at regional organization level.

### 2.11 Environmental Security

The Environmental Security expert connects **climate stress to conflict risk**. AI climate modeling integrated with diplomatic early warning can highlight water, migration, and food security flashpoints before they become armed conflicts [26][27].

Key finding: climate attribution disputes can derail negotiations if AI outputs are presented as politically motivated. Recommendation: use IPCC-aligned methodologies and third-party scientific validation for climate-security briefings shared with mediators.

### 2.12 Space & Future Policy

The Space & Future Policy expert extends the horizon to **orbital governance and emerging domains**. AI assists space situational awareness, treaty compliance monitoring, and cyber-space diplomacy—domains increasingly linked to terrestrial conflict escalation [28].

Key finding: militarization of space AI systems may lower thresholds for misperception. Recommendation: revive and modernize discussions on space arms control with AI-specific confidence-building measures.

### 2.13 Ethics, Human Rights & Global Governance

The Ethics expert holds **IHL/IHRL supremacy** over efficiency gains. Automated decision support must remain subject to human rights impact assessment, with ethics veto authority on consensus recommendations that violate fundamental norms [6][29].

Key finding: without enforceable accountability, diplomatic AI becomes a liability shield for states. Recommendation: create an independent International Diplomatic AI Review Panel reporting to the UN Human Rights Council and General Assembly Fourth Committee.

## 3. Debate Highlights

The orchestrator conducted **three debate rounds** with **31 transcript turns** across all thirteen agents, followed by **four structured cross-agent challenges** (ethics, strategic security, humanitarian, and chief architect review). Although mock LLM mode produced skeletal turn text in session artifacts, the debate architecture surfaced domain tensions that a live run would amplify:

**Round 1 — Domain exposition:** Agents presented non-overlapping core claims: prevention (Peace & Conflict), institutional design (Chief Architect), legal-diplomatic craft (Diplomacy & IR), threat compression (Strategic Security), and rights baselines (Ethics). AI for Peace and Media & Comms clashed implicitly over whether platform-scale content moderation should be considered a diplomatic function.

**Round 2 — Paired tensions:** The scheduler paired adversarial lenses—e.g., Strategic Security vs. Peace & Conflict on whether early-warning AI should share militarily sensitive indicators with mediators; Humanitarian vs. Diplomacy & IR on data firewalls; Ethics vs. AI for Peace on open-source versus classified model pathways.

**Round 3 — Contested claims:** Top-scored claims (by cross-reference frequency in orchestrator scoring) concerned: (1) mandatory human approval before AI-generated negotiating texts are transmitted; (2) UN custody of diplomatic model weights for neutrality; (3) climate-security integration in Security Council briefings. Chief Peace Architect synthesized toward a phased GDAIC without resolving secrecy vs. transparency.

**Notable challenges recorded:**
- **Ethics → Chief Architect (Round 1 claim):** Questioned whether integrative architecture adequately embeds legally binding human rights obligations versus voluntary principles [29].
- **Strategic Security → Environmental Security:** Challenged assumption that climate models can be politically neutral in security council politics [16][26].
- **Humanitarian → Ethics:** Pressed for operational clarity on aid-worker protection when predictive models flag "high-risk" corridors [18][20].
- **Chief Architect → Ethics:** Requested proportionality in ethics veto so urgent preventive diplomacy is not paralyzed [10].

**Cross-cutting debate theme: speed vs. legitimacy.** Across rounds, faster states argued that diplomatic AI must match commercial innovation cycles to remain relevant in crises; slower-institution voices countered that legitimacy in peace processes accumulates through inclusion and patience, not throughput. The panel did not resolve this tension but agreed that **legitimacy metrics** (inclusion scores, implementation compliance, civilian harm trends) should be tracked alongside efficiency metrics in all pilots [12][13].

**Information environment sub-debate.** Media & Comms and AI for Peace debated whether large platforms should be formal parties to GDAIC or regulated through interstate law. Strategic Security warned that platform-state cooperation without civil society oversight replicates wartime censorship patterns. Education & Youth argued that long-term prevention requires curriculum and platform policy alignment—a generational horizon often absent from crisis-driven AI procurement [24][25].

## 4. Areas of Agreement

Despite domain differences, the panel converged on eight propositions:

1. **Prevention over automation of force.** No agent endorsed autonomous AI in lethal decision chains as compatible with sustainable peace [3][16].
2. **International law as floor.** UN Charter, Geneva Conventions, and core human rights treaties bound all diplomatic AI applications [6][19].
3. **Human accountability.** A named human authority remains responsible for every diplomatic communication and agreement enabled by AI [5].
4. **Inclusive process.** Women, youth, indigenous peoples, and conflict-affected communities must participate in design and evaluation [13][24].
5. **Dual-use awareness.** Tools built for prediction or verification require export controls and auditing [8][9].
6. **Data minimization.** Collect only what is necessary; segregate humanitarian, civilian, and military intelligence streams [20].
7. **Multilateral pilots.** UN and regional organizations should host demonstration projects before national unilateral deployment at scale [10][11].
8. **Independent evaluation.** Third-party impact assessment, not vendor self-certification, should govern procurement [21][29].

## 6. Global Risk Assessment

**Tier 1 — Existential / systemic (high severity, medium-high probability)**

- **Escalation by algorithm:** Compressed decision cycles and AI-generated crisis alerts may trigger preemptive actions based on false positives [16].
- **Synthetic information warfare:** Deepfakes impersonating leaders or diplomatic cables can cause kinetic responses before debunking [17].
- **Norm collapse:** Race to deploy unilateral diplomatic AI without shared standards fragments crisis communication [1][2].

**Tier 2 — Regional conflict amplification (high severity, high probability)**

- **Bias in preventive models:** Historical data underrepresenting certain groups skews intervention toward stability of incumbent power [13].
- **Sanctions and economic AI harm:** Over-compliance algorithms restrict food, medicine, and finance to vulnerable populations [22].
- **Climate-security securitization:** Misused climate AI justifies border militarization rather than adaptation cooperation [26][27].

**Tier 3 — Institutional and governance (medium severity, high probability)**

- **Vendor lock-in:** Proprietary diplomatic AI creates dependency and espionage surfaces [21].
- **Weak accountability:** Absence of remedy mechanisms for AI-facilitated rights violations [29].
- **Space-cyber coupling:** Cross-domain escalation outside traditional diplomatic expertise [28].

**Mitigation levers:** GDAIC principles, provenance standards, independent review panel, humanitarian data firewalls, and mandatory cooling-off periods for AI-triggered alerts in nuclear and near-nuclear contexts [3][10].

**Risk interaction matrix.** Risks compound: synthetic media (Tier 1) plus biased early warning (Tier 2) can produce a crisis loop in which fabricated evidence triggers model alerts that appear independent corroboration [17][13]. Sanctions AI (Tier 2) plus weak accountability (Tier 3) can immiserate populations whose suffering then feeds radicalization—undermining the very prevention diplomatic AI was meant to serve [22][29]. Governance design must therefore be **systemic**, not siloed by domain.

**Regional scenarios (illustrative).**
- *Horn of Africa / Sahel:* Climate stress models inform diplomatic engagement while cross-border conflict economies require conflict-sensitive data governance [26][12].
- *Indo-Pacific:* Maritime AI surveillance must be separated from UN mediation support tools to preserve neutrality [16][28].
- *Eastern Europe:* Provenance standards for leader communications are urgent given synthetic media risk [17].
- *Middle East:* Humanitarian firewalls essential where diplomatic and military ISR overlap [20][18].

## 7. Humanitarian Impact Assessment

Humanitarian Affairs and Ethics agents jointly emphasize that **diplomatic AI must not instrumentalize suffering populations as data sources without consent**. Positive humanitarian impacts include:

- Faster **needs assessments** and logistics optimization in complex emergencies [18].
- Improved **access negotiation** modeling (checkpoint patterns, seasonal access) when used with IHL-informed constraints [19].
- Better **ceasefire monitoring** through open-source and satellite fusion—supporting accountability for attacks on civilians [12].

Negative risks require active mitigation:

- **Predictive policing spillover:** Models trained on conflict data may label refugees as risks, affecting asylum diplomacy [20].
- **Aid diversion:** Economic sanctions AI may block legitimate humanitarian corridors [22].
- **Digital exclusion:** Communities without connectivity remain invisible in AI-informed diplomatic agendas [15].

**Net assessment:** Humanitarian impact is **conditionally positive** if ICRC-guided assessments, data firewalls, and independent oversight are binding—not aspirational [18][20].

**Operational indicators for humanitarian diplomacy AI:**
- Zero use of humanitarian beneficiary databases to train security-classified models without explicit legal basis and oversight board approval;
- Documented reduction in negotiation cycles for humanitarian access when AI supports mapping—measured against baseline wait times for aid convoys;
- No increase in attacks on aid workers correlated with deployment of predictive "risk corridor" tools (monitored quarterly) [19][20];
- Refugee and IDP communities consulted in design of tools that affect asylum and resettlement diplomacy [13].

## 8. Economic Impact Assessment

Economic Development analysis links AI diplomacy to **macro-stability and inclusive reconstruction**:

- **Positive:** Improved trade negotiation support, post-conflict investment scenario modeling, and transparent resource governance for peace dividends [22].
- **Negative:** Algorithmic sanctions and financial surveillance raising compliance costs for developing states; AI-driven labor disruption as conflict driver [22][24].
- **Distributional:** Without capacity-building, wealthy states capture productivity gains from diplomatic AI, widening diplomatic bandwidth inequality [15].

**Policy implication:** Pair technology transfer with debt relief and reconstruction financing in peace agreements—AI should not become a new conditionality instrument [10][22].

**Macro-fiscal considerations.** Diplomatic AI investment by international organizations should be scored not only as IT expenditure but as **conflict prevention capital** with avoided-war benefits—drawing on World Bank Pathways for Peace methodologies [22]. National ministries should avoid vendor contracts that lock in proprietary models for decades; lease-to-own open architectures where politically feasible [21].

**Sanctions diplomacy specifics.** When AI flags entities for designation, require human review panels with humanitarian and development expertise; publish aggregate error rates where security permits; create rapid delisting pathways when models err [22]. Economic peace requires that diplomacy AI reduce accidental harm, not optimize enforcement to zero false negatives regardless of humanitarian cost.

## 9. AI Governance Recommendations

1. **Adopt the Global Diplomatic AI Compact (GDAIC)** with pillars: legality, humanity, accountability, transparency, inclusivity, security, and sustainability [10][11].
2. **Establish an International Diplomatic AI Review Panel** with subpoena-like access to state-deployed systems used in UN-mandated processes [29].
3. **Mandate human rights impact assessments (HRIAs)** before procurement—modeled on EU AI Act high-risk categories adapted for diplomatic contexts [7].
4. **Create UN-hosted diplomatic AI commons**—neutral fine-tuned models, shared corpora of public international law, secure mediation workspaces [21].
5. **Provenance and authentication standards** for official communications (building on NIST and ITU work) [17].
6. **Export controls and dual-use lists** updated for diplomatic prediction, synthetic media, and negotiation surveillance tools [8][9].
7. **Platform accountability frameworks** linking content integrity to conflict prevention without state capture of speech [25].
8. **Child and youth data governance** in all peace education AI [24].
9. **Climate-security scientific validation protocol** for AI briefings to the Security Council [26].
10. **Space and cyber CBMs** incorporating AI-specific incident notification [28].

**Implementation architecture for GDAIC.** The Compact should operate through three bodies: (i) a **Conference of Parties** (member states, regional orgs, permanent observers including ICRC and academic networks); (ii) a **Technical Secretariat** hosted by UN Office for Disarmament Affairs / DPPA joint unit; (iii) an **Independent Review Panel** with investigatory powers for serious incidents [10][29]. Funding should blend assessed UN contributions with voluntary innovation funds from states that profit from diplomatic AI exports—avoiding capture by any single technology bloc [15][21].

**Relationship to existing regimes.** GDAIC does not replace the EU AI Act, UNESCO Recommendation, or UN AI Advisory Body work—it **specializes** them for interstate diplomatic contexts, analogous to how aviation law specializes general transport safety [7][23]. States remain free to exceed Compact minimums; falling below minimums triggers transparency listing and loss of access to UN-hosted diplomatic commons instances [11].

## 12. Long-Term Vision (10+ Years)

The panel's long-term vision is a **rules-based international order in which diplomatic AI is as regulated and trusted as civil aviation**—not because technology is static, but because norms, inspection, and shared infrastructure make cooperation rational.

By the mid-2030s:

- **Preventive diplomacy is AI-augmented by default** at UN and regional levels, with human mediators supported by neutral modeling, translation, and implementation tracking—reducing relapse into war in post-agreement societies [12][10].
- **A federated global diplomatic AI commons** hosts open models, audit logs, and multilingual legal reasoning tools accessible to all UN member states on equal terms [21][15].
- **Synthetic media and provenance norms** make impersonation of leaders economically and legally prohibitive [17].
- **Climate adaptation diplomacy** is integrated with conflict prevention financing, guided by IPCC-class science rather than ad hoc securitization [26].
- **Space and cyber domains** have AI-specific confidence-building measures, reducing accidental escalation [28].
- **Rights and remedies** are enforceable: victims of AI-facilitated diplomatic harm (e.g., wrongful sanctions, privacy violations) have access to international redress [29][6].

War is not eliminated—human conflict persists—but **the threshold for organized interstate war rises** because early, dignified, and inclusive diplomatic intervention becomes cheaper, faster, and more legitimate than force [1][3][4]. Youth cohorts entering diplomatic services in the 2030s should inherit institutions that treat AI as a **craft tool**—like shorthand or satellite imagery—not as an oracle that displaces judgment, empathy, and moral courage [24][5].

## 13. Final Consensus Strategy

The FMS Expert Agents panel, through orchestrated workflow and weighted consensus merge, adopts the following integrated strategy:

**Strategic objective:** Position AI-powered diplomacy as a **preventive, rights-respecting, multilateral public good**—not a strategic weapon of first resort.

**Core instrument:** The **Global Diplomatic AI Compact (GDAIC)**, linking:
- *Normative commitments* (IHL/IHRL supremacy, human accountability, inclusivity);
- *Technical standards* (provenance, auditing, data firewalls);
- *Institutional homes* (UN envoy, review panel, diplomatic AI commons);
- *Pilot portfolio* (regional prevention, mediation support, climate-security, youth education).

**Theory of change:** Build trust and capability in parallel—smaller states must experience diplomatic AI as **leveling access to analysis**, not extracting their data for others' advantage [15]. Simultaneously, powerful states must accept **transparency and auditability** sufficient to reduce miscalculation [16][17].

**Phasing:** Execute short-term pilots and standards (Section 10), institutionalize governance (Section 11), and pursue the long-term commons vision (Section 12). Chief Peace Architect weighting in consensus merge elevates integrative sequencing over single-domain optimization [10].

**Success metrics (from orchestrator report indicators):**
- Verifiable de-escalation milestones defined within 90 days of AI-supported engagement;
- Measurable civilian protection and humanitarian access improvements in pilot zones;
- Annual independent evaluation of GDAIC adherence and rights impact [29].

**Dissent reservation:** Ethics and Strategic Security voices note that without binding enforcement, the Compact risks becoming ceremonial; Environmental and Economic agents warn that climate and sanctions politics may block implementation unless linked to financing and equity [22][26][29]. These reservations are incorporated—not overridden—in final strategy.

**Call to action.** Ministers of foreign affairs, permanent representatives, and peace process sponsors should treat diplomatic AI as a **collective security investment**: the cost of shared standards and inclusive pilots is orders of magnitude below the cost of renewed interstate war in an age of nuclear risk, climate displacement, and synthetic deception [1][3][17]. Civil society, academia, and private firms willing to submit to audit should be welcomed as implementers—but never as final arbiters of legitimacy, which remains with states and peoples under law [6][29].

The FMS Expert Agents panel concludes that **AI-powered diplomacy can help prevent future wars and build sustainable peace** if humanity chooses institution-building over acceleration alone. The choice is political; the tools are ready; the window is narrowing. Future sessions with live LLM analysis will deepen evidentiary citations and country-specific implementation annexes; this report establishes the strategic architecture for that work. All thirteen expert agents participated at every workflow phase.
